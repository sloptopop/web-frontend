SP 800-63B-4 · final July 2025, superseding SP 800-63B · mandatory-for-scope · official text (free)

Applies to: US federal digital identity services and relying parties.

§2 Authentication Assurance LevelsClaimants must authenticate at or above the required assurance level; agencies need at least AAL2 when exposing personal information.
§2.1 Authentication Assurance Level 1AAL1 gives basic confidence; verifiers should offer multi-factor options and require proof of authenticator control.
§2.1.1 Permitted Authenticator TypesLists authenticator types permitted at AAL1, from passwords to cryptographic authenticators. Coming soon
§2.1.2 Authenticator and Verifier RequirementsAAL1 authenticators use approved cryptography over authenticated protected channels; federal verifiers use FIPS 140 Level 1 cryptography. We detect

tls.verification-disabledUnverified certificates on a login path mean the claimant cannot confirm the verifier's identity, so the AAL1 channel requirement is unmet.
§2.1.3 ReauthenticationSessions must have a definite overall reauthentication timeout, ideally within 30 days at AAL1. Coming soon
§2.2 Authentication Assurance Level 2AAL2 requires proof of two distinct factors using secure protocols and approved cryptography.
§2.2.1 Permitted Authenticator TypesAAL2 needs a multi-factor authenticator or a physical authenticator combined with a password or biometric. Coming soon
§2.2.2 Authenticator and Verifier RequirementsAAL2 needs approved cryptography, replay resistance, protected channels, and at least one phishing-resistant option offered. We detect

tls.verification-disabledSkipping certificate checks on claimant-to-verifier traffic leaves the verifier endpoint unauthenticated, directly breaching this AAL2 channel obligation.
§2.2.3 ReauthenticationAAL2 sessions need overall timeout, ideally 24 hours, and inactivity timeout, ideally one hour. Coming soon
§2.3 Authentication Assurance Level 3AAL3 gives very high confidence using a non-exportable cryptographic key with phishing resistance.
§2.3.1 Permitted Authenticator TypesAAL3 requires multi-factor cryptographic authentication, or single-factor cryptographic plus password or biometric. Coming soon
§2.3.2 Authenticator and Verifier RequirementsAAL3 demands non-exportable keys, phishing and replay resistance, authentication intent, FIPS validation, and no syncable authenticators. We detect

tls.verification-disabledSkipping certificate checks on claimant-to-verifier traffic leaves the verifier endpoint unauthenticated, directly breaching this AAL3 channel obligation.
§2.3.3 ReauthenticationAAL3 reauthentication must occur within 12 hours overall, with inactivity timeout ideally 15 minutes. Coming soon
§2.4 General RequirementsIntroduces general requirements that apply at every assurance level.
§2.4.1 Security ControlsVerifiers must apply tailored moderate-baseline security controls from SP 800-53 or an equivalent standard. Coming soon
§2.4.2 Records Retention PolicyVerifiers follow records retention rules and assess risk before retaining records voluntarily. Coming soon
§2.4.3 Privacy RequirementsVerifiers apply privacy controls; agencies handle Privacy Act analysis, notices, and consent for extra processing. Coming soon
§2.4.4 Redress RequirementsVerifier and provider must offer easy-to-find complaint redress mechanisms and assess their effectiveness. Coming soon
§2.5 Summary of RequirementsNon-normative table summarising requirements for each assurance level. Coming soon
§3 Authenticator and Verifier RequirementsSets detailed authenticator and verifier requirements; devices may be shared among subscribers with fraud-limiting restrictions.
§3.1 Requirements by Authenticator TypeIntroduces requirements specific to each authenticator type.
§3.1.1 PasswordsDescribes passwords as memorized secrets that must be hard to guess and are not phishing-resistant.
§3.1.1.1 Password AuthenticatorsPasswords are user-chosen or randomly assigned; blocklisted values rejected; no other composition rules imposed. Coming soon
§3.1.1.2 Password VerifiersVerifiers enforce minimum password lengths, accept long and Unicode passwords, forbid composition rules, periodic changes, hints and security questions. Coming soon
§3.1.2 Look-Up SecretsDescribes look-up secrets as stored lists of secrets, such as recovery codes, which are not phishing-resistant.
§3.1.2.1 Look-Up Secret AuthenticatorsLook-up secrets must come from an approved random generator, be delivered securely, and have at least six digits. Coming soon
§3.1.2.2 Look-Up Secret VerifiersLook-up secrets are single-use, stored hashed, salted when short, and protected by rate limiting. Coming soon
§3.1.3 Out-of-Band DevicesDescribes out-of-band authenticators using a separate channel and verifier-generated short-term secret; not phishing-resistant.
§3.1.3.1 Out-of-Band AuthenticatorsOut-of-band channel uses approved encryption, never email, and the device must authenticate itself to the verifier. Coming soon
§3.1.3.2 Out-of-Band VerifiersVerifier authenticates the device without storing its key and exchanges a random secret across channels. Coming soon
§3.1.3.3 Authentication Using the Public Switched Telephone NetworkTelephone-network out-of-band use is restricted; alternatives must be offered and risk indicators considered. Coming soon
§3.1.3.4 Multi-Factor Out-of-Band AuthenticatorsMulti-factor out-of-band authenticators need activation factor each use, and biometric or password data erased afterwards. Coming soon
§3.1.4 Single-Factor OTPDescribes single-factor OTP devices generating one-time passwords from a seed; not phishing-resistant.
§3.1.4.1 Single-Factor OTP AuthenticatorsSingle-factor OTP needs 112-bit key strength, unique nonce, approved generation, and time-based nonces changing within two minutes. Coming soon
§3.1.4.2 Single-Factor OTP VerifiersOTP verifiers protect symmetric keys, use protected channels, accept each OTP once, and rate-limit attempts. Coming soon
§3.1.5 Multi-Factor OTPsDescribes multi-factor OTP generators needing an activation factor; not phishing-resistant.
§3.1.5.1 Multi-Factor OTP AuthenticatorsMulti-factor OTP authenticators require activation each use, plus key strength and nonce rules like single-factor OTP. Coming soon
§3.1.5.2 Multi-Factor OTP VerifiersMulti-factor OTP verifiers protect keys, use protected channels, accept each OTP once, and rate-limit failures. Coming soon
§3.1.6 Single-Factor Cryptographic AuthenticationSingle-factor cryptographic authentication proves key possession; AAL3 requires public-key cryptography; phishing resistance needs extra criteria.
§3.1.6.1 Single-Factor Cryptographic AuthenticatorsAuthentication keys must be protected from disclosure; non-exportable keys need hardware isolation; external authenticators follow connection rules. Coming soon
§3.1.6.2 Single-Factor Cryptographic VerifiersVerifiers use random challenge nonces of 64+ bits, adequate key strength, approved cryptography, and protect keys. Coming soon
§3.1.7 Multi-Factor Cryptographic AuthenticationMulti-factor cryptographic authentication needs a key unlocked by a second factor; AAL3 requires public-key cryptography.
§3.1.7.1 Multi-Factor Cryptographic AuthenticatorsMulti-factor authenticator keys are usable only after activation factor entry each time, with protected key storage. Coming soon
§3.1.7.2 Multi-Factor Cryptographic VerifiersMulti-factor verifier requirements match single-factor ones; output lacking activation flag counts as single-factor. Coming soon
§3.1.7.3 Usage With Subscriber-Controlled WalletsSubscriber-controlled wallets need an activation factor for key access and must erase activation data afterward. Coming soon
§3.1.7.4 Syncable AuthenticatorsSyncable authenticators copy secrets to other devices; specific requirements are in Appendix B. Coming soon
§3.2 General Authenticator RequirementsIntroduces general requirements applying to every authenticator type.
§3.2.1 Physical AuthenticatorsProviders must advise on protecting physical authenticators and allow immediate invalidation when loss or theft is reported. Coming soon
§3.2.2 Rate Limiting (Throttling)Verifiers must cap consecutive failed attempts per authenticator at 100 by disabling it, requiring rebinding. Coming soon
§3.2.3 Use of BiometricsDescribes biometrics, match error rates and the reasons for only limited biometric use.
§3.2.3.1 Biometric AccuracyBiometric systems need a false match rate of 1 in 10000 or better, fixed threshold. Coming soon
§3.2.3.2 Presentation Attack DetectionFacial recognition requires presentation attack detection; voice comparison is prohibited. Coming soon
§3.2.3.3 Injection Attack DetectionBiometric failed attempts are limited, with delays and eventual disabling; sensor integrity should be checked. Coming soon
§3.2.3.4 Use of Biometric SamplesBiometric samples may adapt templates but must be erased promptly afterward, with time limits. Coming soon
§3.2.4 AttestationAttestations conveying authenticator properties must carry a digital signature of at least 112-bit strength. Coming soon
§3.2.5 Phishing ResistancePhishing resistance needs approved cryptography and cryptographic authentication; manually entered outputs do not qualify.
§3.2.5.1 Channel BindingChannel binding ties authenticator output to the protected channel identifier, and verifiers validate that signature. Coming soon
§3.2.5.2 Verifier Name BindingVerifier name binding makes authenticator output cryptographically tied to the authenticated verifier hostname or domain. Coming soon
§3.2.6 Verifier-CSP or IdP CommunicationsSeparate verifier and identity provider must communicate over a mutually authenticated protected channel with approved cryptography. We detect

tls.verification-disabledBackend calls to a CSP or IdP without certificate checks leave the peer unauthenticated, so two-way authentication cannot hold.
§3.2.7 Replay ResistanceAuthentication must resist replay, using nonces or challenges to show freshness. Coming soon
§3.2.8 Authentication IntentThe authenticator itself must establish user intent for each authentication, with explicit action where needed. Coming soon
§3.2.9 Restricted AuthenticatorsRestricted authenticators like PSTN out-of-band need documented risk acceptance, otherwise alternatives must be used. Coming soon
§3.2.10 Activation SecretsActivation secrets need minimum length, stay local, and are limited to ten consecutive failures. Coming soon
§3.2.11 Connected AuthenticatorsAuthenticator-to-endpoint connections must resist eavesdropping, injection and relay, using approved cryptography and direct or protected channels.
§3.2.11.1 Wired ConnectionsWired authenticator connections are assumed trustworthy; users are advised to use trusted hardware. Coming soon
§3.2.11.2 Wireless ConnectionsWireless connections require proximity within 240 meters and an encrypted key established via wire or pairing code. Coming soon
§3.2.11.3 Hybrid ConnectionsHybrid connections use a QR code plus wireless proximity, or cached keying, to reach a tunnel. Coming soon
§3.2.12 Random ValuesRandom values must come from an approved random bit generator with at least 112-bit strength. Coming soon
§3.2.13 ExportabilityNon-exportable keys live in protected hardware and are required at AAL3; syncable keys are exportable. Coming soon
§4 Authenticator Event ManagementIntroduces actions taken for authenticator lifecycle events like binding, loss, compromise, expiry and revocation.
§4.1 Authenticator BindingAuthenticators bind to accounts; providers keep records of them, their characteristics and verification state.
§4.1.1 Binding at EnrollmentBinding at enrollment is treated as part of enrollment, covered in SP 800-63A. Coming soon
§4.1.2 Post-Enrollment BindingHeading only for post-enrollment binding of authenticators.
§4.1.2.1 Binding an Additional AuthenticatorMultiple authenticators may be bound; binding requires adequate authentication and independent subscriber notification. Coming soon
§4.1.2.2 Binding Across EndpointsBinding across endpoints uses a randomly generated binding code over an authenticated protected channel. Coming soon
§4.1.3 Binding to a Subscriber-Provided AuthenticatorProviders should accept subscriber-provided authenticators, binding them like others after establishing their capabilities. Coming soon
§4.1.4 RenewalSubscribers should bind replacement authenticators before expiry; the expiring one should then be invalidated. Coming soon
§4.2 Account RecoveryAccount recovery restores access after authenticator loss and always triggers subscriber notifications.
§4.2.1 Account Recovery MethodsProviders must support at least one recovery method, with alternative methods based on documented risk analysis.
§4.2.1.1 Saved Recovery CodesSaved recovery codes need 64+ random bits, hashed storage, throttled checks, and reissue after each use. Coming soon
§4.2.1.2 Issued Recovery CodesIssued recovery codes need random digits, limited validity per channel, throttled verification, and verified recovery addresses. Coming soon
§4.2.1.3 Recovery ContactsRecovery contacts may receive codes with slightly extended validity; subscribers must be able to view and manage them. Coming soon
§4.2.1.4 Repeated Identity ProofingAccount recovery may repeat identity proofing steps matching the original proofing level. Coming soon
§4.2.2 Recovery Requirements by IAL/AALWhich recovery methods apply depends on identity assurance level and maximum authentication assurance level.
§4.2.2.1 Recovery Without Identity ProofingAccounts without identity proofing recover only through a saved code, issued code, or contact. Coming soon
§4.2.2.2 Recovery at AAL2AAL2 recovery needs two codes, a code plus single-factor authenticator, or repeated proofing. Coming soon
§4.2.2.3 Recovery at AAL3AAL3 recovery mirrors AAL2 for IAL1/2; IAL3 requires a biometric comparison against the original capture. Coming soon
§4.2.3 Account Recovery NotificationEvery account recovery must trigger a notification to the subscriber or their designee. Coming soon
§4.3 Loss, Theft, Damage, and CompromiseCompromised, lost or damaged authenticators must be promptly suspended, invalidated or destroyed; backup reporting method offered. Coming soon
§4.4 ExpirationExpired authenticators must stop working, with expiry indicated on failure and replacement via the binding process. Coming soon
§4.5 InvalidationAuthenticators must be promptly invalidated on account end, request, compromise or ineligibility, with risk-based request checking. Coming soon
§4.6 Account NotificationsAccount events such as recovery must notify stored addresses; at least two addresses supported. Coming soon
§5 Session ManagementIntroduces sessions that persist after authentication and end through timeout, logout or reauthentication.
§5.1 Session BindingsSessions must be bound by a secret issued at authentication; remember-me features cannot replace authentication.
§5.1.1 Browser CookiesSession cookies must be HTTPS-only and narrowly scoped; HttpOnly, SameSite, host prefix and opaque contents recommended. Coming soon
§5.1.2 Access TokensRelying parties must not treat an access token's presence as proof the subscriber is present. Coming soon
§5.2 ReauthenticationSessions need periodic reauthentication, with overall and inactivity timeouts that end the session when they expire. Coming soon
§5.3 Session MonitoringOptional session monitoring for fraud, with its data processing covered by a privacy risk assessment. Coming soon
